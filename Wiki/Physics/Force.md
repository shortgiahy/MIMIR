# Force

**One-liner:** A force is a push or pull that acts on an object, capable of changing its velocity — it is a vector with magnitude and direction.

## Core Idea
$$\vec{F} = m\vec{a}$$
A force is any interaction that causes (or tends to cause) [[Acceleration]]. Forces are vectors — two 10 N forces can add to anywhere between 0 and 20 N depending on their directions. Forces come in two flavors: **contact forces** (require physical touch) and **field forces** (act at a distance through a field).

## Why It Exists
Without the concept of force, there is no framework for explaining *why* objects accelerate. Force is the "cause" in the cause-and-effect story of [[Newton's Second Law]]. It is the bridge from the physical world (pushes, pulls, gravity, electricity) to the mathematical world (acceleration, trajectory, orbit).

## Real-World Applications
- **Structural engineering:** forces on beams and bridges must be calculated to prevent failure — tension, compression, and shear forces all act on structural members.
- **Robotics:** every joint in a robot arm exerts forces and torques; controllers must compute required joint forces to move a payload without exceeding motor limits.
- **Medicine (Baymax context):** soft robotics and inflatable actuators are designed to limit the forces they can exert, making them safe to interact with humans.
- **Electrostatics:** Coulomb force $F = kq_1q_2/r^2$ is a field force — it acts without contact, analogous to gravity but for charges.
- **Aerodynamics:** lift and drag are forces generated by airflow; their balance determines whether a plane climbs, descends, or flies level.

## Intuition
A force is anything that would stretch a spring. Attach a spring scale between two objects; whatever deflects the spring is a force. Its direction is which way the spring is pulled or pushed. Gravity deflects it downward. A push on a box deflects it horizontally. This spring-scale mental model captures both magnitude and direction.

## Derivation
Force is a primitive concept defined by [[Newton's Second Law]] — it cannot be derived from something more fundamental in classical mechanics. Its SI unit, the Newton, is defined as:
$$1\text{ N} = 1\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m/s}^2$$
Forces obey **superposition**: the net effect of multiple forces equals their vector sum ([[Net Force]]). This is not obvious but has been verified experimentally with extreme precision.

**Contact forces** arise from electromagnetic interactions between surface atoms — normal force, friction, tension, spring force, air resistance.

**Field forces** act through fields without contact — gravitational force $\vec{F}_g = m\vec{g}$, electric force $\vec{F}_E = q\vec{E}$, magnetic force $\vec{F}_B = q\vec{v}\times\vec{B}$.

## Worked Example
A box sits on a table. Identify all forces acting *on the box*.

**Step 1 — Gravitational force (field force, downward):**
$$F_g = mg = (5\text{ kg})(9.8\text{ m/s}^2) = 49\text{ N, downward}$$

**Step 2 — Normal force (contact force, upward from table surface):**
Since the box is not accelerating vertically, the normal force must equal and oppose gravity:
$$N = 49\text{ N, upward}$$

**Step 3 — Draw the free-body diagram:**
Two arrows of equal length, one down ($F_g$) and one up ($N$), both acting on the box (represented as a dot).

**Step 4 — Compute net force:**
$$F_{\text{net}} = N + F_g = +49 - 49 = 0\text{ N} \implies a = 0 \checkmark$$
(The box doesn't accelerate — consistent with observation.)
